  strategies with different prizes — they're two routes to the identical 7
  passes. And they measure completely differently: #3 lands 1.249×/1.315× at
  87–100 % of its ceiling; #11b′-combined lands 0.955×–1.011×, i.e. it fails to
  bank a saving it genuinely makes.

  The entire difference is where each one puts the reduction.

  #3 gets rstd for free. The ResAdd kernel already has the full row of h1 in
  registers after the add. Computing Σx² there costs a few FLOPs on resident
  data — and that kernel is memory-bound at 1009 GB/s, 78 % of achievable 
  bandwidth, with essentially zero compute utilization. The arithmetic
  disappears entirely under the memory traffic. There is nothing to displace.

  #11b′ must pay for rstd twice over. It can't compute it in the ResAdd kernel
  (that's #3's trick, and by hypothesis they can't coexist), so it needs a
  separate reduction kernel that re-reads all of h1 — spending a pass to recover
  information #3 got for nothing. Then it applies the scale as an epilogue on
  three GEMMs, and the router GEMM is compute-bound at 76 % of peak with spare 
  bandwidth — the exact inverse profile. There, added work displaces MMA issue
  slots instead of hiding behind memory latency.

  That asymmetry is the whole answer, and #11b makes it quantitative: the
  in-mainloop reduction adds +0.78 % arithmetic and costs −12.3 % of achieved 
  MMA throughput — a 16× amplification. The same handful of FLOPs is free in the
  norm kernel and 16× overpriced in the GEMM.

  So: #3 buys the traffic saving in the kernel that has spare compute; 
  #11b′/#11b buy the same saving in the kernels that have none, and additionally
  spend a pass re-reading h1 to reconstruct what #3 never lost. Same prize,
  strictly worse price — which is why #3 dominates and why no optimal plan in
  the study contains #11b′ despite its standalone 1.03–1.24×.

  This is also exactly why your split-norm proposal is the interesting one: it's
  the only formulation that keeps #3's free reduction and deletes x2, reaching
  6 passes. It still pays the three-GEMM perturbation — which is why its verdict
  hangs on that one w13 epilogue measurement.

  ⚠️ nuance

  Why it matches — the Ampere argument

  C500 is Ampere-class in precisely the way that governs fusion: no warp 
  specialization, no TMA, no thread-block clusters, no DSMEM. Those are
  Hopper/Blackwell features. Ampere-era production practice is "fuse the
  bandwidth-bound vector glue, keep the GEMMs pure and hand them to
  CUTLASS/cuBLAS" — and that is what we measured, without knowing it in advance.

  It also explains the one thing that didn't port. "Towards Free Normalization"
  (the #11 source) depends on warp specialization to park the reduction on
  dedicated warps so it overlaps the MMA pipeline. On C500 that lane doesn't
  exist, so the reduction displaces MMA issue slots instead: +0.78 % arithmetic
  → −12.3 % throughput. The paper isn't wrong; it's Hopper-conditional, and we
  are on Ampere-class silicon.

  irrelevant. Our own control shows the vendor library fusing the same epilogue
  for 0.5–1.7 %. On A100, Triton's epilogue codegen is mature and cuBLASLt fuses
  residual adds routinely. Do not carry 0.846× to A100.

  And one caveat that cuts against our own headline everywhere: we flush L2
  before each measurement, so we price the full cold-DRAM cost of the x2
  round-trip. In a real layer the router reads x2 microseconds after the norm
  wrote it. At decode sizes x2 is only 3 MB — L2-resident on C500, comfortably
  so on A100's 40 MB. Our traffic-saving wins (#3, #10, #11b) are therefore 
  upper bounds, and they'd erode more on A100 than on C500 precisely because
  A100's L2 is 5× larger.

  What does port

  The two things that survive the hardware change are the ones that matter:

  The mechanism. GEMM-mainloop fusion loses on Ampere-class silicon because
  there's no warp specialization to give the fused work an independent issue
  lane — so it displaces MMA slots rather than overlapping them. A100 lacks warp
  specialization exactly as C500 does. vLLM's SM90 gate is production-scale
  agreement.

  The ranking. Vector+vector fusions win; GEMM-mainloop fusions lose; simple
  scalar epilogues are fine but belong in the library, not in hand-written
  Triton. That ordering is architecture-driven, not backend-driven, and it's
  what A100 practice reflects.

  What I would not claim without re-measuring on A100 is any specific ratio. The
  correct summary is: our structure and ranking match A100 practice and are 
  confirmed by vLLM's own SM gating; our magnitudes are C500 numbers and at 
  least three of them are known to be C500-specific.

  Why "like an A100" is the natural but misleading read

  The device presents as Ampere: major=8, minor=0 (SM80), torch.version.cuda = 
  11.6, Triton reports arch=80. That's a deliberate software-compatibility
  surface — MACA gives you a CUDA-shaped API.

  But the hardware underneath tells a different story, and one property gives it
  away: gcnArchName is in the device properties. That's a ROCm/AMD field —
  "GCN" is AMD's architecture family — and PyTorch normally exposes it only on
  HIP builds. Combined with warp_size=64 (AMD's wavefront, never NVIDIA's), 64
  KB LDS, and a 512 KB per-CU register file, C500 is a CDNA-shaped device 
  wearing a CUDA-shaped API. torch.version.hip is None and the backend is its
  own 'maca', so it isn't a ROCm rebuild — but the resource budget is CDNA's,
  not Ampere's.

  Why this matters for the fusion study — quite a lot

  The three constraints that drove our largest findings are all CDNA constraints
  that MI210 shares exactly and A100 does not:

  - 64 KB SMEM — this is what made #6 uncompilable fused (96 KB needed for the
  unfused winner's tile), and what put #11's +4096 B cross-warp reduction
  scratch over the bar on 7 of 79 configs. A100's ~164 KB removes both barriers;
  MI210's 64 KB reproduces them.
  - 8 MB L2 — governs #8's atomic-accumulator crossover. MI210: identical. A100:
  5× larger, crossover moves far up the batch range.
  - warp = 64 — sets the granularity of every cross-warp reduction in #3, #4/#5
  and #11. A100's 32-lane warps change that arithmetic.

  So my earlier caveats need re-pointing: I said #6's SMEM bar was
  "C500-specific." It's more accurate to say it's CDNA-class, and our #6 result
  should port to MI210/MI250 largely intact while genuinely not porting to A100.

  The better production comparator

  If MI210 is the hardware twin, then vLLM's ROCm path — not its CUDA path — is
  the right reference, and it does diverge in exactly the dimension our study

  The trap you'd fall into reading it naively

  gfx90a parses to capability (9,0), so has_device_capability(90) returns True
  on MI210. Read the capability checks alone and you'd conclude MI210 gets the
  Hopper-class paths. What actually excludes it is the is_cuda() conjunct
  sitting beside the capability test. Capability numbers are not vendor-portable
  in this codebase — you must evaluate the platform predicate alongside them.

  What you can't get statically

  Three tiers, and only the first is fully source-derivable:

  Tier 1 — source alone: which ops are fused into which kernels. A pure function
  of platform predicates, the gfx→capability map, library-presence flags, and
  env vars. Evaluate by hand, high confidence.

  Tier 2 — source + arch docs: whether a fusion is even possible. Our #6 needed
  96 KB of LDS for the unfused winner's tile; the CDNA2 ISA guide gives 64 KB
  per workgroup. That's a compile-time impossibility you can prove on paper, no
  hardware needed. Same for register-pressure occupancy bounds.

  Tier 3 — not derivable at all: whether it's faster. This is where our own
  study is the cautionary tale. Fusion #1's regression was invisible to every 
  static signal: registers identical (126 vs 126), zero spills, and the added
  DRAM traffic proven irrelevant (+0.1 %, via a stride-0 broadcast control that
  kept the instructions and removed the traffic). It still cost 22.8 %, purely
  because the epilogue disabled the mainloop schedule that made BK=32, GM≥4
  fast. No amount of source reading or documentation predicts a codegen cliff.

  The caveat that matters most here

  MI210 having no tuned configs at all means vLLM's MI210 path is the fallback,
  not an optimized strategy. So it carries the same weakness you correctly
  identified in the MetaX port: it tells you what vLLM does when nobody has
  tuned for your chip, not what's best for it.

  If you want a production reference that reflects real optimization effort on
  CDNA, MI300X (gfx942) is the one — it has AITER, 21 tuned config files, and
  first-class support. The risk is that MI300X's resources differ from C500's
  more than MI210's do, so you'd be borrowing a strategy tuned for different
  SMEM/L2/CU budgets. That trade — hardware-similar-but-untuned (MI210) vs
  tuned-but-hardware-different (MI300X) — is the real choice, and neither gives
  you a clean answer without measuring.
